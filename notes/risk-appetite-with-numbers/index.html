Risk appetite is a sentence with numbers in it

July 2026 · 5 min read

Most risk appetite statements are unfalsifiable. The company has a low appetite for compliance risk and will not tolerate breaches of applicable law. Nobody disagrees with that. Nobody can act on it either, and — this is the part that matters — nobody can ever be shown to have violated it.

A statement that cannot be breached cannot be supervised. That is not a drafting problem. It is the whole problem.

What a usable statement contains

An appetite statement earns its place when it lets someone answer, without a meeting, whether a specific position is inside or outside it. That requires three things.

A stance — averse, minimal, cautious, open, seeking. This is the qualitative posture, and it is genuinely useful as a direction-setter. It is just not sufficient on its own.

A tolerance in money — the aggregate exposure the category may carry before it requires board attention. Not a target and not a limit that triggers punishment; a threshold that triggers a conversation.

A measurement convention — what counts toward that tolerance, and how it is estimated. Without this, the number becomes an argument about method every time it gets close.

Put together: Cyber and technology risk is stated as averse. Aggregate modeled annual exposure across the category should not exceed eighteen million dollars. Exposure is measured as the modeled annual loss should each risk materialize, before insurance recovery.

That is supervisable. At twenty-five million, the category is outside appetite and the board has something specific in front of it — not a feeling that cyber risk seems elevated.

Two ways a category breaches, and they mean different things

This distinction is worth building into the reporting, because the two situations call for different responses.

A single risk exceeds the tolerance on its own. One exposure is larger than the entire category is meant to carry. That is a concentration problem, and the available answers are narrow: transfer it, treat it down, or accept it explicitly and adjust the tolerance to match reality. What you should not do is leave a stated tolerance that the register openly contradicts — it makes every other number in the document less credible.

The aggregate exceeds tolerance while no single risk does. This is accumulation, and the question for the board is different: was this intended, or did it arrive by drift? Categories creep past tolerance one reasonable decision at a time, and nobody ever decides to be over-exposed. That is precisely why the aggregate needs its own line.

Stance and position have to be reconciled

The most informative signal in a risk report is rarely a breach. It is a category stated as averse that is running at ninety percent of its tolerance.

Technically it is within appetite. Practically, one of two things is true: either the organization is not as averse as it says, or the tolerance was set at a level that does not reflect what averse means. Both are worth surfacing, and both are governance findings rather than risk findings. They say something about whether the framework is describing the business or decorating it.

Flagging that gap costs nothing and tends to produce the most useful discussion in the meeting.

Residual scoring is the softest number, and should be labeled as such

Nearly every framework discounts inherent risk by control strength to get residual risk. Strong controls halve the score; weak controls leave it alone. It is a convention, not a measurement, and it deserves to be stated as one.

The failure mode is circular: the control is rated strong because the risk has not materialized, and the risk is rated low because the control is strong. Nothing in that loop is evidence. Where control effectiveness has actually been tested — an audit, an incident, a near miss — the rating means something. Where it has not, residual scoring is an assertion with arithmetic applied to it.

This is not an argument against residual scoring. It is an argument for putting the mitigation rating and the exposure figure side by side in the report, so a director can see which risks are being held down by a control assumption rather than by a low exposure. Fast-moving risks under weak mitigation deserve their own callout for the same reason: velocity determines how much time anyone has to respond once the assumption fails.

The aggregation trap

One technical caution. Summing exposures across a register assumes independence — that each risk could land in the same year without affecting the others. That is convenient and usually wrong in both directions at once. Some risks are mutually exclusive. Others are correlated, and a single event drives several simultaneously, which is exactly the scenario the aggregate is supposed to warn about.

The practical response is to keep aggregation at the category level, where the correlation story is at least discussable, and to resist producing a single enterprise-wide exposure number from a register. An enterprise figure that looks precise and is built on a sum of independent point estimates is worse than no figure, because it will be quoted. If a board needs one, it should be built from loss data and a stated correlation assumption, not from adding up a spreadsheet.

The test

A risk appetite framework is working when a manager can answer a question about a specific decision by consulting it, rather than by asking the risk function what the board would think. That is a high bar and most frameworks do not clear it.

The ones that do share a common feature: somebody was willing to write down a number that could later be shown to have been exceeded. That takes more nerve than drafting another paragraph about the company's commitment to prudent risk-taking, and it is the only version that does any work.
