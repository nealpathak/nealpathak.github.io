Workflow 01 · Claims reporting

A daily claims pipeline that a board can rely on

From a third-party administrator's loss run to reserving exports, an activity brief, and live executive dashboards, every morning, with a person in the loop only where a person adds something.

The problem as it presents

The administrator sends a spreadsheet of a few thousand claims each day. Someone opens it, fixes the formats, deletes the duplicates, pastes it into a workbook, and emails the totals. The actuary gets a different cut. The dashboards lag by a week and nobody trusts the numbers enough to price a renewal off them. When the person who does this is on leave, it does not happen.

The shape of the solution

  1. An ingest agent parses the file, maps the columns by pattern, standardises every date, amount and label, and validates against a rule set that grew out of the real defects seen over months.
  2. Blocking exceptions quarantine the row and hold the release; warnings are corrected and listed. The exception file goes back to the administrator the same morning.
  3. The standardised listing is reconciled to the prior day, producing the activity brief the claims manager reads over coffee.
  4. Downstream agents produce the actuarial export, refresh the loss development and erosion views, and page the chief risk officer when a policy year crosses ninety per cent of its aggregate.

Where the humans sit

  • An analyst releases a held run, with the exception file already telling them what to do with each row.
  • The analytics lead signs off anything bound for the appointed actuary.
  • The claims manager gets the brief; the chief risk officer gets the alert; the board gets numbers that reconcile to the general ledger.
  • An exception monitor pages the owner when an input is late, a run fails, or a policy denies a write.

Workflow 02 · AI governance

An intake-to-production operating model for AI agents

How an idea for an agent becomes a governed production system, and how it gets switched off. Designed to a standard a regulatory examiner would accept, and sponsored at executive level so the standard holds.

The problem as it presents

Agents are easy to build and easy to sprawl. Within months there are a dozen, each touching data nobody catalogued, sending outputs nobody reviewed, owned by whoever built it, and impossible to describe to an auditor. The alternative most organisations reach for is a blanket ban, which loses the value and does not stop the sprawl.

The shape of the solution

  1. Intake. A one-page proposal: what the agent does, what data it reads, where its output goes, who owns it, what the manual fallback is.
  2. Classification. The proposal is classified by data sensitivity and by whether any output leaves the company. That classification decides the controls required, not a debate each time.
  3. Committee. A standing committee with the chief risk officer, general counsel and the programme lead decides what the agent may do and what data it may touch. The decision is recorded as the agent's authorisation scope.
  4. Build and pilot. Built with tests, run in pilot with every gate on and every run logged, reviewed against the seven controls.
  5. Production. Only when all seven controls pass. Quarterly review; any control failing returns it to pilot.

The seven controls

  • A named owner who can be paged.
  • Data authorisation scoped and enforced at run time, not documented in a policy.
  • A kill switch, with the downstream impact and manual fallbacks known before it is thrown.
  • A per-run audit log: who, what, which data, what the policy engine decided.
  • A human gate before anything leaves the company.
  • Automated tests that must pass before a switch is reset.
  • A documented manual fallback with an owner and an hours estimate.

Workflow 03 · Legal operations

Standardising intake, coverage, funding and vendors on one matter platform

A matter management rollout across a captive and its operating companies, where the hard part was not the software but agreeing what a matter is, who pays for it, and what the vendors owe in return.

The problem as it presents

Each operating company reports matters differently, or not at all. Coverage is decided by whoever is asked. Which entity or policy funds a matter is settled at invoice time, which is the worst time. Outside counsel and the administrator work to whatever guidelines they were last sent. Nobody can say what the group's litigation costs, or which firm is worth what it charges.

The shape of the solution

  1. Intake standardisation. One form, one set of required facts, one identifier. Routing by written rules with the reasoning shown; notices fire on the facts.
  2. Coverage determination. A recorded decision on which line responds, made once, at intake, with the reservation of rights where needed.
  3. Funding. Which entity or policy pays, and at what retention, decided before the first invoice rather than after it.
  4. Vendor workflows. Panel firms and the administrator onboarded to the same guidelines, budgets and reporting; invoices reviewed against them.
  5. Reporting. Budget burn, rate compliance, cycle time and reserves by entity, type and firm, from the platform rather than from memory.

Where the humans sit

  • Entity contacts submit intake; they see the routing decision and why.
  • In-house legal owns coverage and funding decisions and reviews routing recommendations.
  • The captive sees every matter that touches its retention, from day one.
  • Vendors get budgets, guidelines and feedback on invoices, and are measured by the same yardstick.

Workflow 04 · Litigation support

Retrieving and filing court documents without a person watching the portal

A small, complete automation: new filings on a county court portal retrieved, named to the firm's convention, filed to the document system, and the responsible attorney told. Built to run unattended and to fail loudly.

The problem as it presents

Someone checks the portal for each active case, downloads whatever is new, renames it by hand to the naming convention, files it in the right matter folder, and tells the attorney. It takes an hour a day, is easy to get wrong, and is the first thing dropped on a busy day, which is exactly when a deadline-bearing filing lands.

The shape of the solution

  1. A scheduled job enumerates active case numbers from the matter list and checks each on the portal.
  2. New documents are retrieved, classified by type, and named to the convention: date, case, party, document type.
  3. Each is filed to the matter folder in the document system, and the attorney and paralegal are notified with a one-line summary and any deadline the document carries.
  4. Anything the job cannot classify or match goes to a review queue rather than a guess.

What made it hold up

  • Idempotent: a document already filed is never filed twice, so the job can be re-run freely.
  • Loud failures: a portal change or a login problem pages a person within the hour, rather than silently retrieving nothing.
  • The naming convention is data, not code, so the firm can change it.
  • Deadlines extracted from the document are suggestions for the docket, confirmed by a person, never entered automatically.
