When a captive makes sense — and three ways the math lies

July 2026 · 6 min read

Every captive feasibility study I have seen arrives at the same shape of answer: a five-year projection where the captive costs less than the commercial market, surplus accumulates, and the recommendation is to proceed. The projections are usually arithmetically correct. The question worth asking is not whether the numbers add up but whether the comparison was fair in the first place — because three specific assumptions do most of the work, and all three tend to lean the same direction.

The comparison basis is doing the work

A captive earns its keep by avoiding what a commercial carrier charges above expected losses: acquisition cost, general expense, profit margin, and whatever the market cycle is adding this year. That load is real — typically 25 to 35 percent of premium — and capturing it is the entire economic case.

So the single most important number in the model is not the loss ratio or the investment yield. It is the commercial premium you compare against. Set it high enough and the captive wins no matter what else the spreadsheet says.

The discipline here is simple: divide expected losses by the commercial premium and look at the implied loss ratio. If the answer is 50 percent, the model is assuming the carrier is charging double the expected cost, and the conclusion is being manufactured by the comparison rather than earned by the structure. Commercial pricing that implies a 60 to 80 percent loss ratio is defensible. Below that, someone should explain why the market is mispricing this risk so badly — and if it genuinely is, that condition is a feature of the current cycle, not of the captive.

Capital is not free, and it is not a rounding error

The second distortion is subtler because it looks like conservatism. Feasibility models capitalize the captive properly, show the surplus growing, and treat that surplus as a win. What they frequently omit is that the capital had to come from somewhere.

Money posted to a captive at a two-to-one premium-to-surplus ratio is money not deployed in the business. If the enterprise earns a double-digit return on invested capital, charging the captive nothing for the capital it ties up understates its cost materially. On a five-million-dollar program that is two and a half million of capital, and at a nine percent opportunity cost that is well over a million dollars across five years — often a meaningful share of the projected advantage.

The correction is not complicated. Charge the captive an opportunity cost on posted capital at the rate the business actually uses for capital allocation decisions, and let the comparison survive that charge or fail on it. A structure that only works when capital is free is not a structure, it is an accounting preference.

The loss ratio is a single point estimate wearing a suit

The third problem is the one that actually hurts. Feasibility models run at an expected loss ratio — say 65 percent — and produce five clean years. Real programs do not experience their expected loss ratio. They experience a distribution, and long-tail lines experience it slowly, which means a bad accident year does not announce itself until the captive has already written three more of them.

This is where the tail length quietly matters more than the yield assumption. A short-tail program that deteriorates tells you within two years and you can reprice. A seven-year tail program that deteriorates has already committed the balance sheet before the development triangle admits what happened. The investment income that looks so attractive on long-tail business is compensation for exactly that risk, not a free lunch attached to it.

The test I would want to see on any feasibility study is a flat adverse load — twenty points on the loss ratio is a reasonable screen — applied across the whole projection. If the advantage survives it, the structure has genuine margin. If a twenty-point deterioration erases the entire case, the model has been telling you about the expected case and staying quiet about everything else.

What actually makes a captive worth forming

None of this is an argument against captives. It is an argument for knowing which reason you are relying on, because the reasons have very different durability.

Volume is the threshold test. A captive carries fixed cost — management, audit, actuarial, domicile fees — that does not scale down. Below roughly one to two million of funded premium, that fixed base consumes the margin regardless of how favorable everything else looks. This is the first thing to check and the easiest to check.

Stable, predictable frequency is what you want inside. Captives work best on the working layer — the losses you expect to have, priced at their expected cost, funded on your own balance sheet instead of someone else's. Catastrophic severity belongs in the commercial or reinsurance market, which is what those markets are actually good at.

The strategic reasons are often the real ones, and they are legitimate. Direct access to reinsurance markets, coverage for exposures the commercial market prices badly or will not write at all, multi-line diversification credit, and the funding discipline that comes from a structure with its own balance sheet and its own board. These are frequently worth more than the premium arbitrage, and they are more durable, because they do not evaporate when the market softens.

That last point is worth stating plainly. If a captive's case rests entirely on the current hard market, it is a bet on the cycle, and cycles turn. If it rests on structural advantages — access, control, coverage, funding discipline — it survives the market softening, which it will.

The honest version of a feasibility conclusion sounds less like the captive saves four million over five years and more like the captive saves four million if the market holds, one million if it softens to historical averages, and loses money if we see a twenty-point deterioration in the first three years — and here is why we think it is worth doing anyway. That version is harder to present and considerably more useful.
