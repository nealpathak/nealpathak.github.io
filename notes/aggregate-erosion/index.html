The one number to know before the renewal

A captive's board gets a great deal of information before a renewal: loss ratios, open counts, largest claims, a triangle or two. Most of it is context. One number is the decision, and it is the one the carrier's underwriter already has.

The number

For each policy year and line, the aggregate is the most the captive will pay in total before the carrier above it pays. It was set at the last renewal, usually a little above what the year was expected to cost. The number to know is how much of it the year will ultimately use: not what has been paid, not what is reserved today, but what the year will have cost once every claim is closed, as a percentage of the aggregate.

A year at 70% is comfortable. A year at 95% is a question. A year at 110% has already used up its cushion; the carrier has a claim, and the next renewal will price it in whether or not the board noticed.

Why it is not the loss ratio

The loss ratio compares losses to premium. It says whether the year paid its way. It does not say whether the year stayed inside the cover the captive bought, which is a different question with a different consequence. Two years can share a loss ratio and sit on opposite sides of the aggregate, because premium and aggregate are set separately and move separately.

Why it moves

Because the number is an estimate of the future. Claims are reported late; reserves are set low and rise; a single large claim in a young year gets multiplied by a large factor. The standard methods disagree most on the newest year, which is exactly the one the renewal is about. So the honest presentation is not a single figure but a figure with its method and its range: "projected to use 110%, on a method that leans on the pricing assumption for a year this young, and will move."

It also moves for a benign reason: a year at 24 months develops less than a year at 12, so the same claims imply a different ultimate as they age. A board that sees the number quarterly learns its rhythm; a board that sees it once a year sees a surprise.

What to do at ninety per cent

Three things, in order. Put the aggregate carrier on notice early; nothing is gained by waiting and something is lost. Look at what is driving the year: three large claims are a different story from a rise in frequency, and they call for different responses at the renewal. And decide, before the broker does, whether the answer is a higher aggregate, a higher premium, a change in retention, or accepting that this year will pierce and the next will not.

The captive that arrives at the renewal with its own projection, its own driver analysis, and a position on each of those has a negotiation. The one that arrives with a loss ratio has a quote.

What makes the number trustworthy

It rests on the claim file being right, on the methods being the standard ones, and on the assumptions being written down. The methods are not exotic; every actuary uses them. The difference is whether they run on the board's numbers every day, or once a year on someone else's.
