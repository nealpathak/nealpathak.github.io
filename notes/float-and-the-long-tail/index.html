Float is the reward for writing the tail

July 2026 · 5 min read

A medical professional liability book running a 104 percent combined ratio is losing money on underwriting. Every year, reliably, by design. And it can still be a good business, because the premium arrives years before the claims do, and in the interval it earns a return.

That is the standard explanation of float, it is correct, and it is where most discussions of long-tail lines stop. The interesting part is what the explanation quietly assumes, and how often those assumptions get treated as permanent features rather than current conditions.

Float is not free money, it is compensation

The temptation with long-tail business is to treat investment income as a bonus sitting on top of the underwriting result. It is not a bonus. It is payment for a specific service the insurer provides: holding uncertainty for a long time.

A ten-year payout pattern means an accident year is not fully settled until a decade after it was written. Over that decade, inflation moves, legal environments shift, and severity trends do things nobody projected. The float is what the market pays for absorbing that. Describing it as free money is like describing a bond coupon as free money while ignoring duration risk.

This matters because it sets the bar correctly. The question is not does investment income cover the underwriting loss. It is does investment income cover the underwriting loss by enough to compensate for the risk of holding reserves for ten years. Those produce different answers, and only the second one is a real test.

The lag nobody prices

There is a mechanical detail in float economics that gets missed with surprising regularity: premium does not become invested assets the moment it is collected.

It arrives as cash. It sits somewhere earning very little while somebody decides what to do with it. Then it moves into the portfolio. That interval might be a week if the process is tight, or many months if it is not, and during it the money earns a money-market rate instead of a portfolio rate.

On a ten-year horizon that gap compounds into real money. Modeling a book with immediate deployment against the same book with a two-year lag can move cumulative investment income by half — same underwriting, same losses, same yield assumption, entirely different result. The difference is operational, not strategic, and it never appears in an underwriting review because it is not an underwriting number.

The uncomfortable implication is that some of the investment income attributed to skilled portfolio management is really just the absence of an operational delay. And some of a disappointing result is a treasury process nobody thought to examine, because everyone was looking at the combined ratio.

Where the argument turns dangerous

Float justifies an underwriting loss. It does not justify an unlimited one, and the line between those is where long-tail books get into trouble.

The failure mode is gradual and reasonable at every step. The combined ratio drifts from 104 to 108. Investment income still covers it. Then to 112, and rates are up, so investment income still covers it. Each step is defensible against the arithmetic in front of you. The problem is that the arithmetic is built on two things you do not control.

Yields are a market condition, not a business model. A book that works at five percent and fails at three percent is a leveraged bet on interest rates being run by people whose expertise is underwriting. When yields compress, the underwriting loss is still there, and it is larger than it was when the drift started.

Reserve adequacy is an assumption, not a fact. Float only exists if the reserves are right. A long-tail book that has under-reserved is not generating float — it is generating a deferred loss with an investment return attached, and the return is being earned on money that already belongs to claimants. Adverse development on a long-tail line does not arrive as a surprise in year two. It arrives in year six, by which point four more accident years have been written on the same assumptions.

That compounding is the real hazard. Short-tail mistakes surface quickly enough to correct. Long-tail mistakes are repeated several times before the data admits they were mistakes.

What to actually watch

Three things, none of which are the combined ratio on its own.

The operating ratio, at a defensible yield. Combined ratio less investment income as a share of earned premium, stress-tested at a yield well below the current one. If the book only works at today's rates, that should be a stated, deliberate position rather than an accident.

Deployment lag, measured rather than assumed. The interval between collection and investment is an operational metric that shows up as an investment result. Somebody should own it, and it should be a number, not an impression.

Development against expectation, by accident year. Not whether reserves moved, but whether they are moving the way the payout pattern predicted. A tail behaving differently than assumed is the earliest available signal that the float is smaller than it appears, and it arrives years before the financial statements say anything is wrong.

The honest framing of a long-tail book is that it is a financing business wearing an underwriting costume. That is a legitimate business, run deliberately. It stops being legitimate the moment the costume is used to excuse the underwriting rather than describe the economics.
