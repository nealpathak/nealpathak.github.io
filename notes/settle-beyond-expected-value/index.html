The settlement decision, beyond expected value

July 2026 · 5 min read

Put a settlement decision into a decision tree and you get an expected value. The offer is two million; the probability-weighted outcome of trying the case is two point four million; therefore settle. It is a clean answer and it is the right starting point.

It is also incomplete in a way that matters, because expected value describes the average of a distribution that will be experienced exactly once. You do not get to try this case a hundred times and collect the mean. You get one draw.

Two cases, one expected value

Consider two matters that both come out at two point four million expected.

The first is a contract dispute with a narrow damages range. Eighty percent chance of a defense verdict costing five hundred thousand in fees, twenty percent chance of an adverse judgment around ten million. The second is a case with graduated outcomes — a spread of results between one and four million with nothing extreme at either end.

Same expected value. Completely different decisions.

The first case has a twenty percent chance of an outcome that could require a disclosure, a reserve adjustment, and a conversation with the board. The second case has essentially no chance of that. A defendant that treats them identically because the expected values match is not being rational; it is using a summary statistic that has discarded the only feature that distinguishes them.

The number that actually decides it

The most useful figure in a litigation model is not expected value. It is the probability of doing worse than the settlement offer.

That number answers the question a general counsel is actually being asked. Not what is this case worth on average but if we turn down two million, how likely is it that we regret it? A case with an expected value below the offer but a thirty-five percent chance of exceeding it is a genuinely difficult decision. A case with an expected value slightly above the offer but only an eight percent chance of exceeding it is an easy one, in the opposite direction from what the expected value alone suggests.

The second-most useful figure is the shape of the bad tail — not just how likely the worst outcomes are, but how bad they get. Fee exposure, fee-shifting where it applies, the cost of an adverse precedent in a portfolio of similar matters, and the disclosure threshold if there is one. These are the outcomes that turn a litigation decision into a capital decision, and they are exactly what an averaged number hides.

Where the probabilities come from

The obvious objection: these numbers are made up. Trial counsel estimates a sixty percent chance of prevailing on summary judgment, and that estimate carries all the uncertainty of a professional judgment dressed as a decimal.

True, and less damaging than it sounds — for two reasons.

First, the estimates exist whether or not they are written down. Every settlement recommendation contains an implicit probability. Writing it down does not add uncertainty; it exposes uncertainty that was already there and previously unexaminable. When two people disagree about whether to settle, they are usually disagreeing about a specific probability, and the tree tells you which one.

Second, and more useful: sensitivity analysis tells you which estimates matter. Shift each branch probability by ten points and watch the recommendation. Most branches will not change the answer. One or two will. Those are the ones worth arguing about, worth spending discovery on, and worth a second opinion. The rest can stay imprecise without consequence.

That reframing is where most of the value sits. The model does not produce certainty. It identifies which uncertainty is load-bearing.

Two things the tree will not tell you

Portfolio effects. A defendant facing one case should weigh variance heavily; a defendant facing forty similar cases should weigh it far less, because the portfolio averages out what an individual matter cannot. This cuts both ways — settling one case cheaply can be the right call in isolation and the wrong call if it prices the other thirty-nine. A per-matter model cannot see that, and someone has to hold it in mind.

Time. A trial two years out is not the same as a settlement today, and it is not only a discounting question. It is management attention, discovery cost that continues accruing whether or not the case improves, witness availability, and the fact that a case which looks like a coin flip today may look very different after a summary judgment ruling. Some decisions are best made by waiting for the next information event rather than by resolving now — and a tree evaluated today will not suggest that unless the branch is explicitly in it.

What the discipline is worth

The point of modeling a settlement decision is not to remove judgment from it. It is to make the judgment inspectable — to move from counsel recommends settling to counsel recommends settling because there is a thirty percent chance of exceeding the offer, the bad tail runs to eleven million, and the recommendation flips if the summary judgment probability is better than sixty-five percent.

That second version can be reviewed by someone who was not in the room. It can be revisited when the summary judgment ruling arrives, without relitigating the whole analysis. And it survives the departure of the person who made the original call, which the first version does not.
