Risk and legal operations — working software

Your contracts wrote a liability. Nobody added it up.

Contract systems know what the clause says. Risk systems know what the tower covers. Nothing joins them. So the number a board actually needs — the exposure a company's own agreements created, that its own insurance does not answer for — has never been computed. This computes it.

The gap

Three reasons the sum of the caps is the wrong number

Each of these is ordinary. Together they are why a company can hold a complete contract register, a complete schedule of insurance, and still have no idea what it is carrying.

01

The cap is not the ceiling

Almost every negotiated cap carves something out of itself — indemnity, IP infringement, breach of confidentiality, gross negligence. The tidy "1× fees" in the summary field is silent on the four exposures most likely to reach nine figures. Extraction tools record the cap. The carve-out is where the money is.

02

Limits are shared

An aggregate standing behind one contract is standing behind all of them. Reviewing contracts one at a time cannot see the year in which three claims land together and the third one finds the tower already spent. That interaction is the whole reason a programme is bought, and it is invisible to any per-contract analysis.

03

Some perils have no policy

The general liability form excludes infringement of patent, trademark and trade secret. Gross negligence is uninsurable in most states and excluded in any event. Those exposures are not underinsured — nothing was ever written for them. They belong in the retained column from the first day.

What it computes

Every contract is decomposed into the five ways it can turn into money going out the door. Each gets the ceiling that actually applies to it — the cap, the supercap, or nothing. Each is routed to the policy line that answers for it, or to no line at all. Then the whole book is simulated together, with the towers eroding inside every trial, so shared limits behave the way shared limits behave.

The result is a distribution, not a point. What a normal year costs, what a bad year costs, and which agreements were in the room during the worst of them.

What comes out

  • Retained exposure — expected, 1-in-10, 1-in-100, and the average of the worst one per cent.
  • The retained split — how much sits in the retention, how much in the captive, how much above the programme, and how much in classes no form covers.
  • Aggregate exhaustion — the probability each shared limit is spent before year end, and what falls through when it is.
  • A renegotiation list — contracts ranked by their contribution to the tail, not by size. The ten your general counsel should reopen, and what closing each is worth.
  • Risk transfer economics — price a candidate layer and see the cost per dollar of tail removed, next to the levers that cost nothing but a redline.
  • A board memo and full CSV exports, generated from the run.

How it is built

Four commitments that make the number arguable

A risk model that cannot be interrogated is a rumour with a chart on it. These are the properties that let a CFO push back on the answer instead of taking it on faith.

Nothing is uploaded

The register and the schedule of insurance are read in the browser and stay there. There is no server, no account, no log. A risk officer can run their live book on a plane and there is nothing to put in a vendor questionnaire.

The same inputs give the same answer

The simulation is seeded. Re-running a board number six weeks later returns the identical figure, and any movement is a change in the book rather than a change in the dice. The seed is shown on screen and printed in the memo.

Every assumption is on the surface

Claim frequency and severity are visible, editable, and labelled as assumptions rather than dressed up as findings. Where a company has its own loss history, the calibration panel replaces the defaults with it and shows what moved.

The engine is tested, not asserted

Layer allocation, aggregate erosion, cap and carve-out parsing and the reconciliation identities are covered by a self-test that ships in the repository and runs in one command. Losses cannot recover more than they cost and aggregates cannot pay out more than they hold.

Honest limits

What this does not do

  • It does not read contracts. It reads a register. Getting the cap, the carve-outs and the annual value out of the paper is a separate job, and doing it badly makes everything downstream wrong.
  • It does not model coverage disputes, defence costs inside or outside the limit, retroactive dates, or the claims-made trigger. Those change the answer and belong to the next version.
  • It is not an actuarial opinion, and it is not a substitute for one. It is the thing to bring to the actuary so the conversation starts somewhere better than a list of caps.
  • The default frequency and severity assumptions are a starting position for argument. Any company with three years of loss history should replace them, and the tool makes that the easy path.
