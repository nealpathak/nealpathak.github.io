Workflows

How the pieces fit together

A tool on its own changes nothing. What changes a business is the workflow around it: what feeds it, who acts on it, and what happens when it fails. These are four reference designs, described generically, for workflows a legal or risk function should be running. Each says what the business gets, where the people sit, and which of the tools on this site are components of it.

Workflow 01 · Claims reporting

Claims numbers the board can rely on, every morning

From the administrator's daily file to the exports your actuary reads, the brief your claims manager reads, and the dashboards your board reads, with a person in the loop only where a person adds something.

What it costs to run this by hand

An administrator sends a spreadsheet of a few thousand claims each day. Someone opens it, fixes the formats, deletes the duplicates, pastes it into a workbook, and emails the totals. The actuary gets a different cut. The dashboards lag by a week, and the renewal gets negotiated from the carrier's numbers because nobody trusts the internal ones. When the one person who does this is away, it does not happen.

The design

  1. An ingest agent parses the file, maps the columns by pattern, standardises every date, amount and label, and validates against a rule set built from the defects actually seen.
  2. Blocking exceptions quarantine the row and hold the release; warnings are corrected and listed. The exception file goes back to the administrator the same morning, so the file gets better over time.
  3. The standardised listing is reconciled to the prior day, producing the activity brief: new claims, closures, reopenings, reserve movements above a threshold.
  4. Downstream agents produce the reserving export, refresh loss development and aggregate erosion, and alert the chief risk officer when a policy year crosses ninety per cent of its aggregate.

Where the people sit

  • An analyst releases a held run, with the exception file already saying what to do with each row.
  • A named owner signs off anything bound for the appointed actuary.
  • The claims manager gets the brief; the chief risk officer gets the alert; the board gets numbers that reconcile to the general ledger.
  • An exception monitor pages the owner when an input is late, a run fails, or a write is refused.

Workflow 02 · AI governance

Putting AI agents to work without losing control of them

How an idea for an agent becomes a production system the company can defend to a regulator, an auditor or a board, and how it gets switched off when it should be.

The two ways this goes wrong

Agents are easy to build and easy to sprawl. Within months there are a dozen, each touching data nobody catalogued, sending outputs nobody reviewed, owned by whoever built it, impossible to describe to an examiner. The reaction is a blanket ban, which loses the value and does not stop the sprawl. The design below is the middle path: a small, enforced standard with an executive sponsor.

The design

  1. Intake. A one-page proposal: what the agent does, what data it reads, where its output goes, who owns it, what the manual fallback is.
  2. Classification. By data sensitivity and by whether any output leaves the company. The classification decides which controls apply, so it is not renegotiated each time.
  3. Decision. A standing committee of the chief risk officer, general counsel and the programme lead decides what the agent may do and what data it may touch. The decision becomes the agent's enforced scope.
  4. Build and pilot. Built with tests, run with every gate on and every run logged, reviewed against the controls.
  5. Production. Only when every control passes. Quarterly review; a failed control returns it to pilot.

The controls, enforced rather than documented

  • A named owner who can be paged.
  • Data access scoped and checked at run time, not described in a policy.
  • A kill switch, with the downstream impact and manual fallbacks known before it is thrown.
  • A per-run log: who, what, which data, and what the policy engine decided.
  • A person signs before anything leaves the company.
  • Automated tests that must pass before a switch is reset.
  • A documented manual fallback with an owner and an hours estimate.

Workflow 03 · Legal operations

Making legal spend visible across a group

One platform for matters across a group's entities, its captive and its vendors, where the hard part is not the software but settling what a matter is, who pays for it, and what the vendors owe in return.

What it costs to leave it alone

Each entity reports matters differently, or not at all. Coverage is decided by whoever is asked. Which entity or policy funds a matter is settled at invoice time, which is the worst possible time. Outside counsel and the administrator work to whatever guidelines they were last sent. Nobody can say what the group's litigation costs, or whether the firm charging the most is worth it.

The design

  1. Intake. One form, one set of required facts, one identifier. Routing by written rules with the reasoning shown; notices fire on the facts.
  2. Coverage. A recorded decision on which line responds, made once, at intake, with the reservation of rights where needed.
  3. Funding. Which entity or policy pays, and at what retention, decided before the first invoice rather than after it.
  4. Vendors. Panel firms and the administrator onboarded to the same guidelines, budgets and reporting; invoices reviewed against them.
  5. Reporting. Budget burn, rate compliance, cycle time and reserves by entity, type and firm, from the platform rather than from memory.

Where the people sit

  • Entity contacts submit intake and see the routing decision and why.
  • In-house counsel owns coverage and funding decisions and reviews routing recommendations.
  • The captive sees every matter that touches its retention from day one.
  • Vendors get budgets, guidelines and feedback on invoices, and are measured by the same yardstick.

Workflow 04 · Litigation support

Court filings retrieved and filed without a person watching the portal

A small, complete automation: new filings on a court e-filing portal retrieved, named to the firm's convention, filed to the document system, and the responsible attorney told. Built to run unattended and to fail loudly.

What it costs to do by hand

Someone checks the portal for each active case, downloads whatever is new, renames it by hand to the naming convention, files it in the right matter folder, and tells the attorney. It takes an hour a day, is easy to get wrong, and is the first thing dropped on a busy day, which is exactly when a deadline-bearing filing lands.

The design

  1. A scheduled job enumerates active case numbers from the matter list and checks each on the portal.
  2. New documents are retrieved, classified by type, and named to the convention: date, case, party, document type.
  3. Each is filed to the matter folder in the document system, and the attorney and paralegal are notified with a one-line summary and any deadline the document carries.
  4. Anything the job cannot classify or match goes to a review queue rather than a guess.

What makes it hold up

  • Idempotent: a document already filed is never filed twice, so the job can be re-run freely.
  • Loud failures: a portal change or a login problem pages a person within the hour, rather than silently retrieving nothing.
  • The naming convention is data, not code, so the firm can change it.
  • Deadlines extracted from a document are suggestions for the docket, confirmed by a person, never entered automatically.
