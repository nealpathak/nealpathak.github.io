Operating design for regulated work

AI can run workflows.
It cannot run a business.

Certain workflows run without a human. Processes run with one in the loop. Businesses do not run themselves, and a board being told otherwise is being sold something.

So the useful question is not whether the technology works. It is where the line sits — which steps can be handed over, which need a person to confirm before anything moves, and which should never leave human hands regardless of what the tooling can do. That question has an answer, and the answer can be written down, argued with, and tested.

What follows is one operating design for a captive insurance program, with the line drawn explicitly at every step. One node of it is built and running below.

Three levels, and what earns each one

The distinction that matters in insurance and legal work is not how capable a system is. It is what happens when it is wrong — and whether the answer is defensible to a regulator, a court, or a board.

A step earns autonomy by being mechanical, checkable, and cheap to re-run — not by being easy. Some of the hardest computation on this page runs unattended. Some of the simplest judgment does not.

Where the model actually is

A fair objection, and worth answering before it gets asked in a room: much of what is marked autonomous above is not a model at all. Schema mapping, date normalization, chain-ladder arithmetic — software has done that since the nineties, and calling it AI would be the exact overclaiming this page is arguing against.

The model-shaped work sits in three places, and all three are marked human-in-the-loop rather than autonomous: resolving that two records with different identifiers and different spellings are the same claim; reading a claim file against a policy form to surface which provisions are in play; and drafting the board narrative from computed values. Those are the steps where judgment is being approximated, which is precisely why none of them decides anything.

So the argument here is not about which model you use. It is that the governance architecture — declared mappings, exceptions that are held rather than applied, figures that carry their derivation, a log of what was asserted and who agreed — is what makes any of it safe to put in front of a board. That architecture is model-agnostic on purpose. Swap the matcher for something better and every control around it still holds.

The worked example runs deterministically with no live model calls, so it behaves identically every time it is presented and no data leaves the browser. That is a property of the demonstration, not a claim about the architecture.

Why traceability is the whole thing

In this industry the objection is never can it do the work. It is what happens when it is wrong, and can I defend it. A figure a system produces is worth nothing to a board that cannot see what produced it.

So in the worked example the load-bearing figures link to the rows that produced them, every automated resolution is logged rather than applied silently, and exceptions that would flatter the numbers are held rather than taken. The governance panel records what the system asserted and who had to agree — including one conclusion it reached that was wrong, and what caught it.

That last part is deliberate, and it is the part most demonstrations leave out.
