Decisions

Seven decisions, and the number each one needs

A number is only worth having if it changes what you do. Each of these is a decision that gets made with real money, and the working tool that produces the number behind it. The answers shown come from the synthetic book on this site; a real one would come from your own data. If the vocabulary is unfamiliar, the terms page explains each word once, in plain English.

  1. Renewal and capacity

    Will this year's losses fit inside the aggregate we bought, and if not, by how much?

    Developed to ultimate with two standard methods, one policy year and line is projected to use 110% of its aggregate; the shortfall lands in the layer above. Known nine months before the renewal, that is a pricing conversation. Discovered at the renewal, it is a surprise.

  2. Capital

    If next year goes badly, does the capital hold, and what is the cheapest way to make sure?

    Two thousand simulated futures put the chance of breaching the capital requirement at about one in eleven. Holding roughly $700k more brings it under one in twenty. The aggregate cover as priced turns out to cost more certain capital than it protects.

  3. Trusting the claims numbers

    Is the file the administrator sent this morning fit to run the business on?

    Of about 2,500 rows, 12 could not be loaded and 22 were silently wrong; the release is held until a named person clears them. Yesterday's version of this was a spreadsheet nobody checked.

  4. Legal spend

    Which matters will cost more than we approved, and which firm is billing above the rates we agreed?

    Six open matters are projected to exceed budget by $223k combined, before the invoices arrive. One panel firm has billed 7.6% above guideline across three years, with the lines attached to prove it.

  5. Who pays what

    What does risk actually cost each operating company, and what should each pay the captive next year?

    On exposure alone one company would pay its share; on its own record it should pay more. A credibility-weighted allocation moves $142k between five companies next year, capped at 25% from exposure share, and shows which company has been running at a 65% loss ratio while another runs at 80%.

  6. The contracts we signed

    How much revenue sits behind insurance clauses the programme does not actually satisfy, and what is the cheapest way to close it?

    Every live contract's insurance clause joined to the programme the group carries. About a third of contracted revenue requires limits or coverages the group does not have; the first buy-up on the rate card clears the most per dollar, and the certificate draft flags the gap before it reaches the counterparty.

  7. AI in the business

    What are the agents touching, who can switch them off, and what does it cost when they do?

    Ten agents, each with an owner, a data scope enforced at run time, and a gate before anything leaves the company. Switching off the first one idles five others and costs 14½ people-hours a day in manual fallbacks. An examiner could read the log.

All of it lands on one page: the risk committee brief, assembled automatically from the same engines, with the decisions requested at the top.

Tools

Working software on a synthetic book

Each tool runs in your browser from a seed committed to the repository, and ships with tests you can run. Nothing derives from any real insurer, insured, client, or employer; the five operating companies, the panel firms, and every claimant are invented. The methods are the ones your actuary and auditor already use.

  1. 01

    Loss-Run Pipeline

    The administrator's daily claim file, made fit to run the business on: standardised, checked, reconciled to yesterday, and held for a person when something is wrong. Drop in your own file and see what it finds.

    Claims data · Daily · Exports for reserving and reporting
  2. 02

    Loss Development & Aggregate Erosion

    What each policy year will ultimately cost and how much of its aggregate it will use, by line, with the estimate checked against the known answer. The page a renewal should be priced from.

    Reserving · Renewal · Board reporting
  3. 03

    Capital & Cash-Flow Model

    Three years of assets, reserves and capital by quarter, simulated thousands of times. The probability of breaching the capital requirement, the capital that holds it under your tolerance, and whether the reinsurance you are offered is worth its price.

    Capital adequacy · Reinsurance purchase · Dividends
  4. 04

    Agent Control Plane

    The governance layer over a fleet of ten agents running a claims operation. Who owns each, what it may read, where a person signs, what a kill switch costs, and the audit log. Inject a fault and watch a write get refused.

    AI governance · Operations · Examiner-ready
  5. 05

    Matter Portfolio & Intake Triage

    Which open matters will exceed budget, projected from your own history; which firm bills above the agreed rates; which matters have stalled; and where a new matter should go, with the rule that decided it shown.

    Legal spend · Outside counsel · Intake
  6. 06

    Cost of Risk & Premium Allocation

    What risk costs each operating company once everything is counted, who has been paying for whom, and next year's premium allocation with the credibility and the cap as visible knobs. The page that settles the owners' argument with numbers.

    Owners · Total cost of risk · Allocation
  7. 07

    Contract Requirements & Certificates

    Every contract's insurance clause against the programme the group actually carries: the revenue sitting behind requirements it does not meet, the cheapest buy-ups that clear the most of it, the clauses that need a lawyer rather than a broker, and a certificate draft with the gaps flagged.

    Contracts · Risk transfer · Certificates
  8. 08

    Risk Committee Brief

    One printable page assembled from every engine above: decisions requested, claims position, aggregate erosion, capital, legal spend, premium allocation, and AI operations, with the basis of preparation. Nothing on it is typed.

    Executive reporting · Board pack · Print-ready

In practice

What this looks like inside a business

Each of these starts with data the business already has and ends with a number someone can act on, produced by a system that keeps producing it. The tools above are the parts; these are the whole.

Make the claims numbers trustworthy

A daily pipeline from the administrator's file to the exports the actuary, the finance team and the board read, with every defect caught and every release signed. The end of "which spreadsheet is right".

Price the renewal from your own book

Loss development, aggregate erosion and total cost of risk by entity and line, refreshed daily, so the renewal is negotiated from the group's own numbers rather than the carrier's.

Know whether the capital holds

A capital and cash-flow model for a captive or self-insured programme that answers the board's question in one number, and shows what each reinsurance option is actually worth.

Put AI to work without losing control of it

An operating model for agents that do real work: intake, classification, a committee that decides scope, controls that are enforced rather than documented, and a log an examiner can read.

Make the legal function measurable

Intake that routes by rule, budgets that project before the invoice, invoice review against the group's own guidelines, and one platform across its entities and vendors.

Automate the thing one person still does by hand

The court portal, the certificate request, the monthly report. Built to run unattended, to fail loudly, and to hand the decision to a person when it should.

About

Neal Pathak

I came to this work from the legal side: litigation support, transactional drafting, contract management, compliance rollouts. Every function I worked in ran on a few people's memory and a great deal of manual reconciliation, and the software sold to fix it mostly added a place to type things. So I started building the pieces myself: first small automations that did one tedious thing reliably, then the data pipelines and models underneath a risk function, then agents, and then the governance that lets a company trust them.

Today I work in-house across legal, risk and captive insurance, and I lead the design of how AI is used there. The work I like most sits at the joins nobody owns: the contract clause and the policy limit, the loss run and the balance sheet, the agent and the audit log.

This site is built the way I build the tools. Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript; no framework, no build step, no dependencies. Every engine has a self-test and every dataset comes from a seed you can read.
