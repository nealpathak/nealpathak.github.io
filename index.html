Operating design for regulated work

AI can run workflows.
It cannot run a business.

Certain workflows run without a human. Processes run with one in the loop. Businesses do not run themselves, and a board being told otherwise is being sold something.

So the useful question is not whether the technology works. It is where the line sits — which steps can be handed over, which need a person to confirm before anything moves, and which should never leave human hands regardless of what the tooling can do. That question has an answer, and the answer can be written down, argued with, and tested.

What follows is one operating design for a captive insurance program, with the line drawn explicitly at every step. One node of it is built and running below.

Three levels, and what earns each one

The distinction that matters in insurance and legal work is not how capable a system is. It is what happens when it is wrong — and whether the answer is defensible to a regulator, a court, or a board.

A step earns autonomy by being mechanical, checkable, and cheap to re-run — not by being easy. Some of the hardest computation on this page runs unattended. Some of the simplest judgment does not.

Why traceability is the whole thing

In this industry the objection is never can it do the work. It is what happens when it is wrong, and can I defend it. A figure a system produces is worth nothing to a board that cannot see what produced it.

So in the worked example every number links to the rows behind it, every automated resolution is logged rather than applied silently, and the governance panel records what the system asserted, how confident it was, and who had to agree. Including one place where it was wrong, and what caught it.

That last part is deliberate. A pipeline that only shows its successes is not a pipeline anyone has actually run.
