What a legal department should cost

July 2026 · 6 min read

Legal is one of the last functions where the budget conversation still happens without a unit cost. Finance has cost per transaction. Operations has cost per unit. Legal has a number that went up again this year, an explanation involving a large matter, and a request to hold it flat next year.

The reason is not that legal work resists measurement. It is that the two ways of buying it — hiring someone or retaining a firm — are usually compared on incompatible terms. Outside counsel arrives as a rate. In-house arrives as a salary. Those are not the same kind of number, and the entire make-versus-buy question turns on converting them into one.

The in-house effective rate

The conversion is straightforward: fully loaded annual cost divided by productive hours, grossed up for the management overhead any added headcount creates.

Both inputs get abused. Fully loaded means salary, bonus, benefits, payroll taxes, allocated occupancy, technology, and support — commonly 1.3 to 1.4 times base salary. Comparing a base salary against an outside rate is the single most common way this analysis gets rigged toward insourcing, and it is usually accidental rather than dishonest.

Productive hours is the input that decides the answer and gets the least scrutiny. An in-house lawyer does not bill. They also attend management meetings, handle escalations, supervise outside counsel, and do the twenty-minute questions that never become matters. Sixteen hundred productive hours a year is a defensible planning assumption. Two thousand is a fantasy that will make any insourcing case look better than it is, and it fails quietly — nobody notices the assumption was wrong, they just notice the department seems busier than the model predicted.

At three hundred and fifty thousand fully loaded and sixteen hundred hours, the effective rate is about two hundred and twenty dollars, or roughly two hundred and forty with overhead. Against outside rates of five to eight hundred, the arithmetic looks decisive. It usually is. But rate is only the first of two tests.

The half-lawyer problem

The second test is the one models routinely skip, and it is where insourcing business cases leak.

Suppose IP work runs nineteen hundred hours a year and thirty percent of it could sensibly be handled internally. That is five hundred and seventy hours, or about a third of an attorney. The rate comparison says insource — you would save several hundred dollars an hour. But you cannot hire a third of a lawyer. You either hire a whole one and carry two-thirds of their capacity against work that does not exist, or you do not hire at all.

A model that multiplies hours by a rate differential will book that saving anyway. It will produce a headcount plan with 0.33 FTE in it, and the resulting budget will be wrong in a way that is difficult to detect until the hire is made.

So insourcing needs to pass both tests: the rate has to favor it, and the demand has to support at least half an attorney at realistic utilization. Work that passes on rate but fails on volume is not a saving — it is a flag. Sometimes several such matter types can be pooled under one generalist, which is a real and often overlooked answer. Sometimes the honest conclusion is that this work stays outside until volume grows.

Consolidation is pricing power, not administration

The second lever is the panel, and it is usually framed as a governance exercise — fewer firms, easier billing compliance, more consistent rates. Those are benefits. They are not the point.

The point is that rate concessions are priced off the volume a firm expects to gain or lose. A firm receiving four percent of your work has no reason to discount, because the relationship is not material to them. A firm receiving twenty-five percent of it, with the prospect of thirty-five, has every reason. Consolidating from fourteen firms to six typically earns something in the range of five to ten percent on retained spend — and unlike a hiring plan, it delivers in one negotiation cycle with no ramp, no recruiting risk, and no severance exposure if you are wrong.

The sequencing point matters more than the percentage: decide the panel size first, then negotiate. Asking for a discount and then deciding how much work to send is asking a firm to price an option you have not committed to. The leverage exists only when the volume is credible.

What the model cannot tell you

Three things sit outside any spreadsheet, and pretending otherwise is how these exercises lose credibility with the people who have to live with the result.

Litigation is volume risk, not steady state. A single significant matter can move total spend more than an entire consolidation program saves. Budgeting litigation alongside contract review as if they behave the same way produces a number that will be wrong every year. It belongs in its own line with its own variance assumption.

Specialist depth is not fungible with generalist capacity. The model says the hours are interchangeable at a rate. They are not. There is work where the outside firm's specific experience is the product, and insourcing it saves money right up until the one matter where it does not.

Transition has costs that never appear. Recruiting time, ramp, the institutional knowledge sitting with the firm you just cut, and the internal capacity consumed running the change. A twelve-month saving projection that ignores the first six months of transition is describing year two and calling it year one.

The number worth publishing

Where this lands is a defensible unit cost: total legal spend as a share of revenue, and cost per matter by type. Not because those benchmarks are precise — they vary enormously by industry and litigation profile — but because they turn an annual argument about a lump sum into a conversation about drivers. Volume went up. Mix shifted toward litigation. Rates moved. Realization against the internal effective rate slipped because productive hours fell.

That last one is worth tracking deliberately. The insourcing case is built on an assumption about productive hours, and if those hours quietly erode — more meetings, more escalations, more twenty-minute questions — the effective rate rises and the case weakens without anyone revisiting it. Checking it annually costs nothing and is the difference between a model that stays true and one that was true once.
