One insurance workflow, run twice

Renewing a captive takes about 17 weeks and $62,000. The same renewal, run as a pipeline with people at the gates, takes about 8 weeks and $16,000.

Below is the annual renewal of a captive insurer, seven steps from the first data request to the bound policy, on a synthetic captive with five operating companies and $15m of premium. Each step is shown as it is usually done, and then as a pipeline: deterministic engines for the numbers, a language model where there is text to read or write, and a person at four named gates. Every artifact is produced live from the demo data. Every hour, rate and fee is an assumption you can change at the bottom of the page.

Seven steps on one axis

Grey is the renewal as usually run; red is the pipeline. The pipeline's remaining length is mostly waiting: the reinsurance market takes what it takes to quote, and the board meets when it meets. Change either at the bottom of the page and both bars move.

The workflow

Each step, both ways

For every step: who does the work today and for how long, what the pipeline does instead, where a person signs, and the thing that gets produced, computed from the demo book as you read. Where a language model is used, the exact prompt and its recorded output are there to open. Where one is not, the step says so.

Where the AI is

Six uses of a language model, and what the rest is

Most of the saving comes from the pipeline and the engines: code that does the same thing every day, with a test. The model earns its place where the input is prose and the output is prose: five messy replies, adjuster notes, term sheets, a contract. Each use is listed with the gate after it. The site has no backend, so the runs are recorded: made once, committed with their prompts, and checked by the test suite against the engines so that every number in the text is still the engines' number.

What it does not change

Read this before the headline

The market's clock. Reinsurers take weeks to quote and a board meets on its date. The pipeline compresses everything around that waiting; it cannot compress the waiting. Of the 38 business days in the pipeline track, 25 are waiting.

The actuary. The statutory opinion at year end is unchanged and outside this workflow. What changes is the renewal analysis: the actuary reviews and opines on what the engines produced, at a smaller fixed fee, rather than building it from a data package.

Commission, fronting, taxes. Not in either column. They are the same either way.

The baseline is a model, not a measurement. Hours times rates is an estimate of what the renewal costs today. If yours is cheaper or dearer, the table above is where to say so.

The build. The pipeline costs something to build and something to run, and neither is in the per-cycle figure. On the defaults it pays for itself in about 2 renewals on the renewal alone. The same pipeline produces the monthly claims reporting for the other eleven months, which is where the larger return sits.

The data. Every figure comes from a synthetic book generated from a seed in the repository. No real insurer, insured, claimant, administrator, reinsurer or employer is described. The book is evaluated at policy-year end, 31 August 2026, and the renewal is modelled from it; a live renewal would evaluate at 31 May and project the last quarter, which changes nothing about the process.

The reinsurance finding. On this book, the cover that was being renewed on autopilot turned out not to be worth its price at today's capital, and the board was given a real choice instead. That is a property of this synthetic book and its volatility assumption, not a general truth about aggregate stop-loss. It is on the page because it is the kind of thing a computed renewal finds and a hand-assembled one does not.

About

I came to this work from the legal side: litigation support, transactional drafting, contract management, compliance. Every function I worked in ran on a few people's memory and a great deal of manual reconciliation, and the software sold to fix it mostly added a place to type things. So I started building the pieces myself: first small automations that did one tedious thing reliably, then the data pipelines and models underneath a risk function, then the language-model steps, and then the gates that let a company trust them.

Today I work in-house across legal, risk and captive insurance, and I lead the design of how AI is used there. This page is built the way I build the real thing: plain HTML and JavaScript, no framework, no build step, every engine with a self-test, every dataset from a seed you can read, every model run recorded with its prompt.
