Retention is a capital allocation decision

July 2026 · 5 min read

Retention decisions usually get made the wrong way round. The broker brings options — here is the premium at a two hundred and fifty thousand dollar retention, here it is at five hundred thousand, here is what you save by going to a million — and the conversation becomes an evaluation of premium credits. Whichever option saves the most money against a budget line wins.

That framing quietly misstates what is being decided. Raising a retention does not save money. It converts a fixed, known cost into a variable, uncertain one, and hands the difference to the balance sheet to absorb. Whether that is a good trade depends entirely on what the balance sheet charges for absorbing it — which is a capital allocation question, not an insurance procurement question.

The premium credit is the easy half

The cost of a retained program has four parts, and only the first is on the broker's spreadsheet.

Expected retained losses are the honest baseline. If you retain the first five hundred thousand of every claim, you should expect to pay some amount every year, and that expectation is knowable from loss history with reasonable confidence. Trading a premium for an expected loss of the same size is a wash, not a saving.

Frictional cost arrives whether or not losses do. Claims administration on retained claims, collateral posted to the fronting carrier — commonly a letter of credit at a fee of one percent or so on a substantial share of expected retained losses — plus the internal time to manage a program you now partly own. None of this shows up in the premium comparison, and together it is rarely trivial.

The capital charge is the part almost nobody prices, and it is the one that should drive the answer. Somewhere on the balance sheet there has to be capacity to absorb a bad year. Not the expected year — the bad one. The distance between what you expect to pay and what you might pay is capital that is effectively committed to this program, and that capital has an opportunity cost equal to whatever the business earns on capital it deploys elsewhere.

Volatility cost is the fourth part and the hardest to put a number on: the disruption when a bad year forces a decision nobody wanted to make at that moment. It rarely gets modeled. It is often the reason a CFO says no to an otherwise sound recommendation, and treating that as irrationality rather than as an unpriced input is a mistake.

Why the efficient point is rarely the highest retention

Run the total cost across a range of retentions and the curve has a characteristic shape. It falls quickly at first, because the early increments of retention buy back premium that was heavily loaded relative to the losses in that layer — working-layer losses are the most expensive thing to insure, precisely because they are the most predictable. Insuring predictable losses means paying someone a margin to hand your own money back to you.

Then it flattens. Then it turns up.

It turns up because past a certain point you are retaining severity, not frequency. The premium you buy back gets thinner — there is not much expected loss above a two million dollar attachment — while the capital you have to hold against the tail keeps growing. You are paying more for capacity than you are saving in premium.

The efficient retention is where that curve bottoms out, and it is almost always well short of the highest option on the table. The instinct that more retention equals more savings holds only while you are buying back frequency.

Measure the tail properly, or the curve will not turn

One technical point matters more than it sounds. If you measure the tail with a percentile — the ninety-fifth percentile outcome, say — the curve often refuses to turn up, because a percentile stops moving once the retention exceeds most of the severity distribution. It tells you where the bad years start, not how bad they get.

Charging capital against the average of the outcomes beyond that percentile fixes this. It responds to how heavy the tail is, not merely where it begins, and a fat-tailed exposure correctly gets charged more capital than a thin-tailed one with the same percentile. Under that measure the curve behaves the way intuition says it should, and the recommended retention moves down as severity variability rises — which is the correct direction and, notably, the opposite of what the premium-credit framing suggests.

The two questions worth asking

Almost everything above collapses into two questions that a risk committee can actually answer.

What does capital cost here? Not the cost of debt — the return the business expects on capital it deploys. A company earning fifteen percent on invested capital should be far more reluctant to tie capital up behind a retained program than one earning six. Expensive capital argues for transfer. It is a genuinely different answer for a growth business than for a mature one, and using the same retention philosophy across both is how you end up with a structure that fits neither.

How bad a year can we absorb without changing plans? This is a real number, and someone in finance already knows it. It is the point at which a loss stops being a line item and starts being a conversation about the capital plan. Once it is stated, it acts as a constraint rather than an afterthought: no structure whose bad-year cost exceeds it should be recommended, regardless of how attractive its expected cost looks.

Framed that way, the retention decision stops being a procurement exercise and becomes what it always was — a decision about how much of the company's own capital to put behind its own risk, and what it expects to earn for doing so. That is a conversation the CFO should be in, and the version where you compare premium credits is a conversation that keeps them out of it.
